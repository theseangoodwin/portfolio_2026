In the beginning of my career I worked as an identity designer. Gradually I moved into digital products as a "visual designer," where I worked alongside an information architect that would spec out wireframes. Later, when the industry collapsed both roles into the "product designer," I still found it common to move from low to high fidelity in many of my projects.

There was a natural, sequential process of design artifacts moving from whiteboards or wireframes (low fidelity) to pixel perfect mockups and interactive prototypes (high fidelity). Producing high fidelity used to take time, attention, and effort. Now, we (and our PMs) can generate a realistic prototype in minutes. How should we think about prototype fidelity in an AI-native world?

## Benefits of fidelity
<span class="fidelity-highlight fidelity-highlight-first">Pre-AI, fidelity often advanced with problem definition.</span> *Definition* is how clearly a team understands the problem and its chosen direction. Whether the process started from an existing flow or a blank canvas, the collaborative friction gave teams time and space to reflect together. This enabled shared understanding and alignment.

<!-- FIDELITY_DIAGRAM -->

Fidelity also minimized risk for the business. Code was expensive; therefore the business needed conviction a design was "validated" before building. High fidelity experiences communicated a concrete vision before an expensive team of engineers began reproducing it. Critically, stakeholders could sign off on an approved version of the experience to demonstrate a shared alignment.

## AI-native pitfalls
Now I am seeing a pattern on AI-native teams: they start in high fidelity prototypes. <span class="fidelity-highlight">They often skip over problem definition work, preferring to generate another iteration instead.</span>

<!-- FIDELITY_DIAGRAM_SECOND -->

The teams run into issues of misalignment, delays, and churn. They spend time untangling assumptions by working backwards from each other's proposed solutions. Teams anchor to early solutions without sufficiently considering other approaches. The assumptions underneath are untested while the prototype looks complete and polished.

Generating detail is now incredibly easy with AI, but evaluation has become the bottleneck. Simply producing more prototypes in high fidelity is a slow way to improve your team's alignment on what matters. After being on the barrel-end of enough "slop cannons," I've realized receiving more high fidelity AI outputs without clear problem definition is liable to introduce more confusion.

## Deliberate fidelity choices
Most prototypes communicate or provoke a decision. Yes, generative prototyping can speed up design production. But we shouldn't confuse conceptual sketches and blueprints. Fidelity is a design tactic, and deploying the right level of abstraction should increase your learning and decision velocity.

User flows, storyboards, sketches, whiteboards, and wireframes remain useful in an AI-native design process. Low fidelity enables your team to move at high velocity in the stages of early definition. You focus on the right problems while trusting that the details can be filled in later.

High fidelity is useful when people must experience the details to evaluate a solution. For a narrow or incremental change, that may be the fastest place to start. But many teams move directly to implementation and sidestep problem definition. The risk is moving 10x as fast in the *wrong direction*.

## Shaping the work
Low fidelity can keep exploration focused and enable you to fill in the details later. A practical use of low fidelity comes from [Ryan Singer](https://x.com/rjs)'s book on product development, *[Shape Up](https://basecamp.com/shapeup)*.

![Illustration of shaping work at the right level of abstraction](../images/high-fidelity-low-definition/shaping-the-work.png)

> When we shape the work, we need to do it at the right level of abstraction: not too vague and not too concrete. Product managers often err on one of these two extremes.
>
> <footer>Ryan Singer, <cite><a href="https://basecamp.com/shapeup">Shape Up</a></cite></footer>

Ryan describes the issue of developing concrete (ie, high fidelity) artifacts too early as "*leaving no room for creativity*". An interpretation of this is to prevent ourselves from anchoring. The psychological effect of anchoring is that your later decisions are influenced by your starting point. When you anchor too early, you hit an opportunity cost of finding more creative and innovative ideas, and direct costs of time and energy overcommitting to the wrong direction.

Low fidelity helps you explore in two ways:
1. Low fidelity keeps focus on the most important aspects of the problem. When a sculptor works in clay, they add material to ensure the overall form is clear before introducing details like facial expression or muscle tone. Extraneous details will not solve foundational issues.
2. Low fidelity keeps idea generation **and** evaluation cheap. Anticipate the evaluation bottleneck by intentionally reducing elements. Said more simply, this is the design version of "[Why waste time say lot word when few word do trick.](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/rhBqlDsqMAk)"

Shape your product like a sculptor; develop the form and then decide the details.

## Determining your own fidelity
Define what you must learn or decide. Consider who is your audience. Then, select the fidelity most suited to the task. If you want to compare high level journeys, then a user flow or storyboard can suffice. When sharing any concept, [high fidelity will get you low level feedback, and low fidelity will get you high level feedback](https://x.com/round/status/1151500868593836038). Evaluating the product system is easier when you abstract away the implementation details. However, if your scope is narrow and well defined, moving straight to high fidelity prototyping may be the fastest way to evaluate a new experience.

The next time someone sends you a polished prototype without clear direction, ask "What decision will this help us make? What do we need to learn from this prototype?" If the details don't help answer, step down into low fidelity approaches like user flows or whiteboard sketches as a 'visual shorthand' to speed up collaboration. Encourage your team to focus on the most critical aspects first. And once direction and scope are clear, ramp up the prototype fidelity.
